The Resume Is a Formula, Not an Art Project

September 26, 2026 · career, resumes

Recruiters spend 7 seconds on a first scan. Three things fix 90% of resumes: a template, a word list, and a formula.

Most resumes fail for boring, fixable reasons. Not because the person isn't qualified — because the document makes the recruiter do work.

Recruiters spend roughly 7 seconds on a first scan. In 7 seconds, nobody is reading your story. They're hunting for one thing: proof you deliver results. Everything on the page should either be that proof or get out of the way.

Three things fix about 90% of resumes. A template, a word list, and a formula.

1. The container: Jake's Resume

Jake's Resume is the one-page LaTeX template half of CS uses, and there's a reason it won. It's clean, dense, ATS-friendly, and it enforces the discipline most people need: one page, no photos, no colors, no clever layouts.

The template is not the point, but it removes an entire category of failure. Nobody ever got rejected because their resume used Jake's template. Plenty of people get rejected because their resume looks like a design project. Pick the boring template and spend your energy on the words.

2. The words: Harvard's action verbs

Harvard's career services office publishes categorized lists of action verbs — leadership, communication, research, technical — and one core rule: every bullet starts with a strong verb.

The verbs doing the most damage on real resumes: "Responsible for" — the single worst opener. It describes a job description, not an achievement. "Helped with" / "Assisted in" — tells the reader you were nearby when work happened. "Worked on" — says nothing.

Replacements that actually say something: spearheaded, built, shipped, reduced, scaled, led, designed, optimized, launched. Pick the verb that matches what you actually did, then make the rest of the bullet earn it.

Harvard's broader guidance is worth stealing too: active language over passive, ruthlessly skimmable formatting, and — via Laszlo Bock — roughly one page per ten years of experience. Your resume's job is to get the interview, not to get you hired. Once you're in the room, the resume barely matters.

3. The formula: Wonsulting's XYZ

This is the one that does the heavy lifting. Wonsulting's XYZ formula:

Accomplished [X], as measured by [Y], by doing [Z].

X is the outcome. Y is the number that proves it. Z is how you did it. Every bullet gets all three, or it gets rewritten.

Before: "Responsible for backend services for the payments team." After: "Cut payment processing latency 40% (250ms → 150ms p99) by migrating the ledger service to an event-driven architecture."

Before: "Worked on machine learning models." After: "Improved fraud detection recall 12 points (81% → 93%) at constant precision by replacing hand-tuned rules with a gradient-boosted model over transaction embeddings."

Same work. Different resume. The second version answers the only question the recruiter is asking: so what did you actually do?

No exact numbers? Estimate honestly, or quantify the scale instead: users served, requests per second, team size, budget. "Served 2M daily requests" is a Y even without a before-and-after. A vague bullet with scale beats a specific-sounding bullet with nothing.

Wonsulting took the recruiter scan seriously enough to personify it: Wendy is their AI recruiter persona, and her criteria are this post's checklist — strong verbs, quantified outcomes, zero fluff. Write a resume that survives her 7-second scan and it survives a human's too.

Put it together

The system is unglamorous, which is why it works: 1. Pour everything into Jake's template. 2. Open every bullet with a Harvard-grade action verb. 3. Rewrite every bullet until it fits XYZ: outcome, number, method.

Do that and your resume stops being a list of duties and starts being evidence. Seven seconds is plenty — when every line is proof.
