LinkedIn is a beacon, not a resume

September 27, 2026 · career, linkedin

Four things fix about 90% of profiles. A headline, a story, the proof, and the polish.

Most LinkedIn profiles fail for boring, fixable reasons. Not because the person isn't impressive — because the profile gives a recruiter nothing to find.

A Meta recruiter found me on LinkedIn.

I did not apply. I did not message anyone. I did not even know the role existed. My profile did the applying for me, while I was asleep, because it was built to be found.

Here is the thing most people get backwards: a resume is a document you send. LinkedIn is a beacon you maintain. One works when you push it. The other works while you sleep. And 95% of recruiters use LinkedIn to find candidates — they are searching right now, for someone like you, and your profile is either the answer or invisible.

Four things fix about 90% of profiles. A headline, a story, the proof, and the polish.

1. The headline: not your job title

Your headline is the most-read sentence you will ever write. It shows up in every search result, every comment, every connection request — usually just the first 60 characters. And most people spend it restating their job title, which the recruiter can already see one line down.

Wonsulting's pattern: [role] | [key skill] + [key skill] | [differentiator].

Before: "Software Engineer at TechCorp." After: "Backend Engineer | Distributed Systems & Event-Driven Architecture | Cut payment latency 40%."

Same person. One version is a label; the other is a reason to click. Front-load the 60 characters a stranger actually reads, and stuff the remaining 160 with the keywords a recruiter would search.

2. The story: your About section

Nobody reads your About section. They read the first two lines — everything before "see more" — and decide whether to continue. So the first two lines are the whole game: one hook that says what you do and what outcome you drive.

Then 2–3 proof points. Then a closing line that invites the message: what you're looking for, or just "happy to connect."

First person, short paragraphs. Nobody reads a wall of text on a phone, and "I am a passionate results-driven professional" is where attention goes to die.

Before: "I am a passionate software engineer with a proven track record of delivering results in fast-paced environments." After: "I build backend systems that move money. At TechCorp I cut payment processing latency 40% and redesigned a 13-year-old email system into event-driven microservices. I write about what I learn along the way — happy to connect."

3. The proof: your Experience

This is the easy one, because you already did the work. Take the XYZ bullets from your resume — accomplished [X], as measured by [Y], by doing [Z] — and copy them straight into each role, numbers included.

Recruiters don't read Experience top to bottom; they search keywords and skim for numbers. Every bullet with a metric is a search hit and a skim-stop. A role with no bullets is a role that doesn't exist.

4. The polish: one afternoon

The rest is an afternoon's work, and almost nobody does it, which is exactly why it compounds:

Photo: real face, plain background, face filling most of the frame. No logos, no landscapes, no wedding-crop.

URL: linkedin.com/in/yourname. The default string of numbers looks unfinished.

Skills: list up to 50, hard skills first — they mirror the keywords in the job descriptions you want.

Featured: pin your best proof. A project, a post, a talk. 80% of profiles leave this empty; filling it is a free edge.

Recommendations: ask. Managers, teammates, anyone who saw the work. People almost never refuse, and third-party proof hits different than self-reported proof.

Put it together

The system is unglamorous, which is why it works: write a headline that says what you deliver, not where you sit; tell your story in the About — hook, proof, invitation; copy your XYZ bullets into Experience; and spend one afternoon on the polish.

Do that and your profile stops being a static resume and starts being a beacon. You cannot control which recruiter searches tomorrow, or what role opens up next quarter. You can control whether they find you when they do.
