Obsession

September 27, 2026 · career

A Meta recruiter found me on LinkedIn. I didn't apply. That was the part that happened after years of showing up at the bus stop.

Everyone wants the ending where the bus comes to them.

This is the part about the years walking to the stop.

A Meta recruiter found me on LinkedIn.

I did not apply. I did not write a cover letter about how Meta had been my lifelong dream. I did not ask for a referral. I did not lock myself in a room for six months and turn LeetCode into a personality trait.

They came to me.

That looks like luck. It was luck. But it was not the beginning. It was the part that happened after years of walking to the bus stop.

Before Meta, Amazon, Citi, or Lockheed Martin, I was at Virginia Commonwealth University trying to transfer to Georgia Tech.

That was the hardest part.

I had no momentum. No impressive logo. No recruiter in my inbox. No career story that made strangers assume I knew what I was doing.

I just had to work.

I studied and pushed until I became a strong enough transfer candidate. There was no shortcut and no one to pull me through. It was the smallest version of the snowball, so I had to supply almost all the force myself.

People see the snowball when it is already flying downhill. They do not see you at the top, sweating, trying to move something the size of a grapefruit.

Georgia Tech gave it weight. Then I needed an internship.

My path into Lockheed Martin did not begin at a polished networking event. It began with a friend's mom from elementary school.

Networking is usually sold as name tags and awkward small talk. My version was simpler: I looked around, found someone who might help, and asked.

Shamelessly.

Most people do not have "no network." They have a network they have not learned to see: classmates, neighbors, friends' parents, old group chats.

The network is usually already there. The hard part is asking.

Lockheed became Citi. Citi gave me a case study: design a financial dashboard. I did not open Figma. I did not spin up a framework. I picked up my iPad and drew the whole thing by hand — boxes, charts, layout, sketched out like a storyboard. Scrappy, unpolished, completely legible. That is why I got the job.

Citi became Amazon. Each step was earned, but each one made the next one easier.

Credentials compound. Once one recognizable company bets on you, the next one has less uncertainty to overcome. One logo makes the next conversation easier.

The snowball gains mass.

At first I pushed hard just to create motion. Later, momentum started helping. Recruiters replied faster. Interviews got easier to get. Instead of convincing people I might be capable, I had a trail of companies that had already decided I was.

The work did not disappear. It changed form.

Early on, effort meant chasing opportunities. Later, it meant positioning myself so opportunities could find me. LinkedIn stopped being a static resume and became a beacon. My roles, my work, the way I told the arc — it all told the market what I wanted next.

Then Meta found me.

I joined my current team because the work looked interesting. That was honestly the calculation. Interesting problems, smart people, good fit. I did not have a ten-year plan.

Then the work turned out to be important enough that people I used to work with now use what my team builds.

I picked an interesting team. The team became important. My old colleagues became users. The snowball kept rolling, and suddenly I was somewhere I could not have mapped from the beginning.

From the outside, this can look like failing upward. Stumbling from one opportunity into another until one of the biggest companies in the world recruits you without an application.

That version is funnier. The honest version is simpler: obsession.

Nobody fails upward seven times in a row.

From the inside, it looked like compounding on purpose. Work created a credential. The credential created access. Access created another chance to do good work. That work created reputation. Reputation improved positioning. Positioning attracted luck.

Then the luck looked effortless, because everyone could see the outcome and almost nobody watched the accumulation.

I did not control which recruiter would message me, when a company would hire, or whether an interesting team would become an important one. I could only improve the odds of being in the right place when something opened up.

Luck is like going to the bus stop every day.

You cannot control when the bus arrives. Sometimes it is late, full, or drives straight past you. That feels personal. It probably is not.

But if you never walk to the stop, you have turned uncertainty into certainty. You are definitely not getting on.

So I kept walking to the stop. I transferred. I asked the elementary-school mom. I took the internship. I stacked the credentials. I kept my profile visible. I chose work that seemed interesting, even when I could not predict where it would lead.

I walked to the stop for years.

Eventually, the bus rerouted to my house.

Go stand at the stop.
